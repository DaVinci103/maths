Cardinals

Cardinals

In mathematics, a cardinal refers to the number of elements of a given set. Given a set $X$, its cardinality is denoted $|X|$ or $\#X$. The cardinality $|X|$ is a cardinal. For sets $X$ and $Y$, we have $|X| = |Y|$ iff there is a bijection $f: X \leftrightarrow Y$.

One might notice that the collection of cardinals, along with cardinal identity $|X| = |Y|$, forms the data of a set: the rules reflexivity, symmetry and transitivity are satisfied by the identity function, inverses and function composition. However, the collection of cardinals is "too large" to be a set: none of the constructions discussed in the previous section can make a set large enough to serve as the set of cardinals. Although currently we do not have the necessary machinery to actually prove the collection of cardinals isn't a set, I can give a short outline of the proof: we assume there is a set $C$ of cardinal, aiming for a contradiction. We define a larger (well, it's technically not smaller or equal, not strictly larger) set $\aleph(C)$ and define, for each $\alpha \in \aleph(C)$, a cardinal $\aleph_{\alpha}$ such that $\aleph_{\alpha}$ and $\aleph_{\beta}$ are different cardinals whenever $\alpha \neq \beta$. From this, it follows there are at least $|\aleph(C)|$ cardinals, meaning that $|\aleph(C)| \le |C|$, while that's impossible by construction of $\aleph(C)$.

Collections that are similar to sets that are too large to be sets themselves are sometimes called classes. However, since these collections are really just the same as sets appart from their size, I'll refer to them as large sets in this section.

Given a universe $\Univ$, we have a set $\Card_{\Univ}$ of $\Univ$-small cardinals (cardinalities of sets in $\Univ$). This set $\Card_{\Univ}$ is not in $\Univ$ itself, so it is a $\Univ$-large set. From this point onwards, we fix a universe $\Univ$. We refer to sets in $\Univ$ as small sets, sets outside of $\Univ$ as large sets and we write $\Card$ for $\Card_{\Univ}$. In HoTT, $\Card_{\Univ}$ is formally defined as the set-truncation of the groupoid (1-type) of $\Univ$-small sets.

The main examples of cardinals are $n = |\mathbf{n}|$, the cardinality of the $n$-element set, $\aleph_0 = |\NN|$, the cardinality of the set of natural numbers, and $\cc = |\pow(\NN)|$, the continuum. We often use Greek letters in the middle of the alphabet, such as $\kappa,\lambda,\mu$, to denote cardinal numbers.

Cardinal Arithmetic

For cardinals $\kappa = |A|$ and $\lambda = |B|$, we define:

\[\begin{array}{rcl} \kappa + \lambda &:=& |A+B| \\ \kappa \cdot \lambda &:=& |A \times B| \\ \kappa^{\lambda} &:=& |B \Rightarrow A| \end{array}\]

For example, $\cc = 2^{\aleph_0}$. We sometimes write $\kappa \lambda$ for $\kappa \cdot \lambda$. Order of operations is as usual. We have the following basic identities:

\[\begin{array}{rclrcl} (\kappa+\lambda)+\mu &=& \kappa+(\lambda+\mu) & (\kappa\lambda)\mu &=& \kappa(\lambda\mu) \\ \kappa+\lambda &=& \lambda+\kappa & \kappa\lambda &=& \lambda\kappa \\ \kappa(\lambda+\mu) &=& \kappa\lambda+\kappa\mu & (\kappa^{\lambda})^{\mu} &=& \kappa^{\lambda\mu} \\ \kappa^{\lambda}\cdot\kappa^{\mu} &=& \kappa^{\lambda+\mu} & \kappa^{\mu}\cdot\lambda^{\mu} &=& (\kappa\lambda)^{\mu} \end{array}\]

By Cantor's diagonal argument, we have $\lambda \neq 2^{\lambda}$. Suppose, aiming for a contradiction, there is a bijection $f: A \leftrightarrow \mathcal{P}(A)$. Define $S \subset A$ by $S = \{x \in A \mid x \notin f(x)\}$. By definition of bijection, there is some $s \in S$ for which $f(s) = S$. Suppose $s \in S$. By definition of $S$, we have $s \notin f(s)$, but by definition of $s$, $f(s) = S$ and therefore $s \notin S$, contradicting $s \in S$. Therefore, $s \in S$ can't be true, and we have $s \notin S$. By definition of $s$, we have $f(s) = S$, and therefore $s \in f(s)$, and by definition of $S$, we therefore have $s \in S$. A contradiction arises and no such bijection $f$ exists.

We say a cardinal $\lambda$ is idempotent iff $\lambda \cdot \lambda = \lambda$. Examples of idempotent cardinals are $0$, $1$ and $\aleph_0$. The cardinal $2$ is not idempotent, as $2 \cdot 2 = 4 \neq 2$. Tarski's theorem on idempotent cardinals states that the axiom of choice is equivalent to every infinite cardinal being idempotnent. We'll prove this in a future chapter.

Cardinal Comparison

For cardinals $\kappa = |A|$ and $\lambda = |B|$, we define:

  • $\kappa \le \lambda$ iff there is an injection $f: A \hookrightarrow B$;
  • $\kappa \nleq \lambda$ iff not $\kappa \le \lambda$;
  • $\kappa < \lambda$ iff $\kappa \le \lambda$ and $\lambda \nleq \kappa$.

For cardinals $\kappa$, $\lambda$ and $\mu$, if $\kappa \le \lambda$ and $\lambda \le \mu$, then $\kappa \le \mu$: we simply compose the underlying injections. This property of $\le$ is known as transitivity. The relation $\le$ is also reflexive: for any cardinal $\kappa$, we have $\kappa \le \kappa$.

The Cantor-Shröder-Bernstein theorem states that $\le$ is antisymmetric. That is, if $\kappa \le \lambda$ and $\lambda \le \kappa$, then $\kappa = \lambda$. We'll see this in the a future chapter.

Totality of $\le$ states that, for any two cardinals $\kappa$ and $\lambda$, we have $\kappa \le \lambda$ or $\lambda \le \kappa$ (or both). Totality is not automatic. If $\kappa \le \lambda$ or $\lambda \le \kappa$, then we call $\kappa$ and $\lambda$ comparable, otherwise they're incomparable. Totality of $\le$ is equivalent to the axiom of choice, this can be proven through the use of Hartog numbers.

We say a cardinal $\kappa$ is infinite iff it is not finite, i.e. not $n = |\mathbf{n}|$ for any finite $n$. Equivalently, $n \le \kappa$ for all finite $n$. What is not equivalent is $\aleph_0 \le \kappa$. A cardinal $\kappa$ for which $\aleph_0 \le \kappa$ is known as a Dedekind infinite cardinal. If the axiom of choice holds, then infinite cardinals and Dedekind infinite cardinals coincide. You'll be asked in the exercises to prove several equivalences of forms of the definition of a Dedekind infinite cardinal.

For cardinals $\kappa = |A|$ and $\lambda = |B|$, we define:

  • $\kappa \le^* \lambda$ iff there is a surjection $f: B \twoheadrightarrow A$;
  • $\kappa \nleq^* \lambda$ iff not $\kappa \le^* B$;
  • $\kappa <^* \lambda$ iff $\kappa \le^* \lambda$ and $\lambda \nleq^* \kappa$;
  • $\kappa =^* \lambda$ iff $\kappa \le^* \lambda$ and $\lambda \le^* \kappa$.

Note that a surjection $f: B \twoheadrightarrow A$ realizes an $A$-indexed partition of $B$: the cells of the partition are the fibres $f^{-1}\{a\}$ of $f$. We can thus think of $\kappa \le^* \lambda$ as $\lambda$ has a $\kappa$-partition.

Just like $\le$, the relation $\le^*$ is reflexive and transitive. However, it doesn't need to be antisymmetric. If $\kappa =^* \lambda$, then that doesn't directly imply $\kappa = \lambda$.

One consequence of the definition of $\le^*$ I have given is that $0$ is maximal: for any set $A$, if there is any function $\varnothing \to A$, then $A$ is empty. In other words, if $0 \le^* \kappa$, then $\kappa = 0$. This might be an undesirable property of $\le^*$, so sometimes the definition is modified to: $\kappa \le^* \lambda$ iff there is a surjection $f: S \twoheadrightarrow A$ for some $S \subset B$. I'll simply assume $\kappa$ and $\lambda$ are non-zero whenever using $\le^*$.

For non-zero $\kappa$ and $\lambda$, we have that $\kappa \le \lambda$ implies $\kappa \le^* \lambda$, so the relation $\le^*$ is weaker than $\le$. The converse statement, that $\kappa \le^* \lambda$ implies $\kappa \le \lambda$, is known as the partition principle. The axiom of choice implies the partition principle: if $f: B \twoheadrightarrow A$ is a surjection, then a section of $f$ is an injection $A \hookrightarrow B$. However, whether the partition principle implies the axiom of choice is still an open problem.
