Cantor Shröder Bernstein

Given two cardinals $\kappa = |A|$ and $\lambda = |B|$ it can sometimes be difficult, or just tedious, to given an explicit bijection between the underlying sets $A$ and $B$ to show that $\kappa$ and $\lambda$ are equal. In this chapter, we will prove a theorem that will make it easier to prove when two cardinals are equal. In the main body of this chapter, as well as in the exercises, we will look at some applications of this theorem. This theorem is known as the Cantor-Shröder-Bernstein theorem. The proof given is König's proof of the theorem. The result does not use AC, but is also not constructive (i.e. uses LEM). There is a much stronger result on the ordering of cardinals if we do allow use of the axiom of choice, known as the well-ordering principle, which will be discussed in a few chapters.

Cantor-Shröder-Bernstein

The statement of the theorem is as follows: let $A$ and $B$ be sets. Let $f: A \hookrightarrow B$ and $g: B \hookrightarrow A$ be injections. Then, there is a bijection $h: A \leftrightarrow B$. Restated in terms of cardinals: let $\kappa$ and $\lambda$ be cardinals. Suppose $\kappa \le \lambda$ and $\lambda \le \kappa$. Then, $\kappa = \lambda$.

The proof of this theorem is not too difficult. Since $f$ and $g$ are injections, they have partial inverses $f^{-1}: \im f \to A$ and $g^{-1}: \im g \to B$, where $\im f \subset B$ and $\im g \subset A$ are the image of $f$ and $g$ respectively. For each $a \in A$, we can define a sequence $(a_0,b_1,a_2,b_3,\ldots)$ of elements alternating between elements of $A$ and elements of $B$. First, we set $a_0 := a$. Then, assume $a_n$ has been defined, and define $b_{n+1} := g^{-1}(a_n)$. Assume $b_n$ has been defined, and define $a_{n+1} := f^{-1}(b_n)$. The sequence terminates when $g^{-1}(a_n)$ or $f^{-1}(b_n)$ is undefined. These sequences parition $A$ into three sets: $S_0 \subset A$, of $a \in A$ for which the sequence is infinite (and possibly runs in a cycle), $S_1 \subset A$, of $a \in A$ for which the sequence ends in an element $a_n$ of $A$ (i.e. the sequence has odd length), and $S_2 \subset A$, of $a \in A$ for which the sequence ends in an element $b_n$ of $B$ (i.e. the sequence has even length). We can do the same for $B$. For $b \in B$, we define $(b_0,a_1,b_2,a_3,\ldots)$ by $b_0 := b$, $a_{n+1} := f^{-1}(b_n)$ and $b_{n+1} := g^{-1}(a_n)$. Then, we partition $B$ into $T_0 \subset B$ of elements for which the sequence is infinite, $T_1 \subset B$ for which the sequence ends with an element of $A$ (i.e. is of even length), and $T_2 \subset B$ for which the sequence ends with an element of $B$ (i.e. is of odd length). Then, $f|_{S_0}: S_0 \leftrightarrow T_0$ is a bijection between $S_0$ and $T_0$, and $g|_{T_0}: T_0 \leftrightarrow S_0$ is a bijection between $T_0$ and $S_0$. The function $f|_{S_1}: S_1 \leftrightarrow T_1$ is a bijection (for each $b \in T_1$, $(b_0,a_1,\ldots)$ has even length, therefore cannot terminate in $b_0$, therefore $a_1$ exists and $f(a_1) = b_0$), however $g|_{T_1}: T_1 \hookrightarrow S_1$ is only an injection. And, similar, $f|_{S_2}: S_2 \hookrightarrow T_2$ is only an injection, but $g|_{T_2}: T_2 \leftrightarrow S_2$ is a bijection. We can glue these restricted functions together into a bijection $h: A \leftrightarrow B$,

\[h(a) := \left\{\begin{array}{ll} f(a) & a \in S_0 \\ f(a) & a \in S_1 \\ g^{-1}(a) & a \in S_2 \end{array}\right.\]

In the first case, we could've chosen either $f(a)$ or $g^{-1}(a)$, as both are bijections $S_0 \leftrightarrow T_0$. In the latter two cases, only specifically $f$ is guaranteed to be a bijection $S_1 \leftrightarrow T_1$, and only $g^{-1}$ is guaranteed to be a bijection $S_2 \leftrightarrow T_2$. The bijection $h$ proves the theorem.

Application: $\RR \leftrightarrow \pow(\NN)$

We want to show that $\RR$, the set of real numbers, and $\pow(\NN)$, the powerset of the natural numbers, have the same cardinality. By the Cantor-Shröder-Bernstein theorem, it suffices to show there are injections $f: \RR \rightarrowtail \pow(\NN)$ and $g: \pow(\NN) \rightarrowtail \RR$.

First, $f: \RR \rightarrowtail \pow(\NN)$. We know that there are as many rational numbers as natural numbers, i.e. $|\QQ| = |\NN| = \aleph_0$. Therefore, showing there is an injection of $\RR$ into $\pow(\NN)$ is equivalent to showing there is an injection of $\RR$ into $\pow(\QQ)$, which is what we'll do. Note that a real number $x$ is uniquely determined by what rational numbers are less than it: if reals $x$ and $y$ are distinct, then $x < y$ or $y < x$, so assume, WLOG, $x < y$. Between any two real numbers is a rational number, so there is some $q \in \QQ$ with $x < q < y$. This means that $q$ is less than $y$, but not less than $x$. We can thus define the injection $f$ by mapping $x$ to

\[\{q \in \QQ \mid q < x\}\]

Second, $g: \pow(\NN) \rightarrowtail \RR$. We can view a subset $A \subset \NN$ as a function $A: \NN \to \{0,1\}$, or an infinite sequence $(a_0,a_1,a_2,\ldots)$ of $a_n \in \{0,1\}$. We can map this sequence to the decimal, or maybe ternary, expansion $0.a_0a_1a_2\ldots$. Note that a binary expansion wouldn't work here, as e.g. $0.1010111111\ldots_2 = 0.1011000000\ldots_2$, so the function wouldn't be injective.

The Cantor-Shröder-Bernstein theorem now gives us a bijection $h: \RR \leftrightarrow \pow(\NN)$, showing that $|\RR| = \mathfrak{c}$.

Exercises

Let $\kappa$ be an infinite cardinal, i.e. $n \le \kappa$ for all finite $n$. Recursively define $\beth_0(\kappa) := \kappa$ and $\beth_{n+1}(\kappa) := 2^{\beth_n(\kappa)}$. Show that $\beth_4(\kappa)$ is idempotent (recall: a cardinal $\lambda$ is idempotent iff $\lambda \cdot \lambda = \lambda$).

Let $\kappa$ and $\lambda$ be cardinals with $\kappa \le \lambda$ and $\lambda$ idempotent. Prove that $\kappa^{\lambda} = 2^{\lambda}$.
