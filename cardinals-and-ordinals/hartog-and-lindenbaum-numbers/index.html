Hartog and Lindenbaum Numbers

In the previous chapter, we looked at cardinal arithmetic with the axiom of choice. In this chapter, we'll introduce Hartog numbers and Lindenbaum numbers, which are useful tools for studying cardinals without the axiom of choice. Explained simply, a Hartog or Lindenbaum number of a (possibly not well-orderable) cardinal $\kappa$ is a well-orderable cardinal of approximately the same "size", or representing the "reach", of $\kappa$. We'll use Hartog numbers to prove cardinal trichotomy implies AC, and Tarski's theorem on the axiom of choice.

Hartog and Lindenbaum Numbers

Given a set $X$, its Hartog number $\aleph(X)$ is the set of well-orders of subsets of $X$, quotiented by isomorphism, ordered under the existence of proper segments. In other words, it is the smallest ordinal $\alpha$ for which there is no injection of $\alpha$ into $X$. This Hartog number $\aleph(X)$ is an initial ordinal: if there were to exist an $\alpha < \aleph(X)$ with $|\alpha| = |\aleph(X)|$, then the injection of $\alpha$ into $X$ composed with a bijection between $\alpha$ and $\aleph(X)$ (witnessing $|\alpha| = |\aleph(X)|$) gives an injection of $\aleph(X)$ into $X$, a contradiction. Recall we identify initial ordinals with their cardinalities, so $\aleph(X)$ can be alternatively defined as a cardinal number. The Hartog number $\aleph(\kappa)$ of a cardinal $\kappa = |X|$ is defined as (the cardinality of) the Hartog number of $X$.

The Lindenbaum number $\aleph^*(X)$ of $X$ is the set of well-orders of quotients of $X$, quotiented by isomorphism, ordered under the existence of proper segments. In other words, it is the smallest ordinal $\alpha$ for which there is no surjection of $X$ onto $\alpha$. The Lindenbaum number $\aleph^*(X)$ is initial for (almost) the same reason $\aleph(X)$ is. The Lindenbaum number $\aleph^*(\kappa)$ of a cardinal $\kappa = |X|$ is defined as the Lindenbaum number of the underlying set $X$. It is clear that, for non-zero $\kappa$, we have $\aleph(\kappa) \le \aleph^*(\kappa)$.

If $\kappa = \aleph_{\alpha}$ is an Aleph-cardinal, then $\aleph(\aleph_{\alpha}) = \aleph^*(\aleph_{\alpha}) = \aleph_{\alpha+1}$ is simply the successsor cardinal of $\kappa$.

A cardinal $\kappa$ is called Dedekind of mediate iff it is infinite and Dedekind-finite, i.e. $n \le \kappa$ for all finite $n$, and $\aleph_0 \nleq \kappa$. We have that $\kappa$ is Dedekind iff $\aleph(\kappa) = \aleph_0$, as you'll prove in the exercises. If $\kappa$ is Dedekind, then $\aleph^*(\kappa)$ can still be arbitrarily large

The Lindenbaum number of the reals is denoted by $\Theta := \aleph^*(\RR)$.

Hartog Trichotomy

By the well-ordering principle, the axiom of choice implies cardinal trichotomy. That is, for any two cardinals $\kappa$ and $\lambda$, we have exactly one of $\kappa < \lambda$, $\kappa = \lambda$ or $\lambda < \kappa$. The converse can be proven using Hartog numbers.

Suppose, for all cardinals $\kappa$ and $\lambda$, we have $\kappa \le \lambda$ or $\lambda \le \kappa$. Then, the axiom of choice holds.

To show this, let $\kappa$ be any cardinal and let $\lambda = \aleph(\kappa)$. Then, either $\kappa \le \aleph(\kappa)$ or $\aleph(\kappa) \le \kappa$. By the definition of the Hartog number, we have that $\aleph(\kappa) \nleq \kappa$, and therefore $\kappa \le \aleph(\kappa)$. Subsets of well-orderable sets are well-orderable, so $\kappa$ is a well-orderable cardinal. This proves the well-ordering principle.

Tarski's Theorem

Tarski's theorem on the axiom of choice states that, if every infinite cardinal is idempotent ($\lambda \cdot \lambda = \lambda$ for infinite $\lambda$), then AC holds. This can be proven using Lindenbaum numbers.

Suppose every infinite cardinal is idempotent. Let $B$ be an infinite set. We'll show $B$ has a well-order. Let $\beta = \aleph^*(B)$ be the Lindenbaum number of $B$. We have that $|B + \beta|$ is idempotent, i.e. we have a bijection $f: B + \beta \leftrightarrow (B + \beta)^2$. Since $B \nleq^* \beta$, we know that, for each $x \in B$, there is some element of $\{x\} \times \beta \subset (B+\beta)^2$ not in the image of $f|_B$. Therefore, as $f$ is bijective (and, in particular, surjective), there is some ordinal $\gamma$ for which $f(\gamma) \in \{x\} \times \beta$. Denote the least such $\gamma$ by $g(x)$. Then, $g$ is a function from $B$ to $\beta$. We can see $g$ is injective. Therefore, $|B| \le |\beta|$, and $B$ is well-orderable.

Exercises

Recursively define $\beth_0(\kappa) = \kappa$ and $\beth_{n+1}(\kappa) = 2^{\beth_n(\kappa)}$. Show that $\aleph^*(\kappa) \le \beth_3(\kappa)$.

Let $\kappa$ be an infinite cardinal. Show that TFAE:

  1. $\kappa$ is Dedekind-finite;
  2. $\aleph(\kappa) = \aleph_0$.

A set $A$ is amorphous iff it is infinite and for all $S \subset A$, either $S$ is finite or $S$ is cofinite (i.e. $A \setminus S$ is finite). Show that the powerset of an amorphous set is Dedekind.

Prove that $\aleph_1 \le \aleph(\RR)$ and $\aleph_2 \le \Theta$.

Suppose, for all cardinals $\kappa$ and $\lambda$, we have $\kappa \le^* \lambda$ or $\lambda \le^* \kappa$. Show that the axiom of choice holds.

Let $\alpha$ be an infinite initial ordinal. Define an injection $f: \alpha \times \alpha \hookrightarrow \alpha$. Conclude that AC implies idempotence of infinite cardinals, proving the converse of Tarski's theorem.
